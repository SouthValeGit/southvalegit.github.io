Module 1: How Instagram Reels actually get distributed

You cannot design videos that spread until you understand what the platform is measuring. Instagram does not publish its full ranking formula. It has, however, said publicly which signals matter most for Reels. Those signals line up with what every successful short-form creator has learned the hard way.

The signals that matter

1. Watch time and completion. Did people watch the whole thing? Did they watch it twice? A 20-second reel watched to the end by 70% of viewers will beat a 60-second reel watched to the end by 15%. This is the most important number you will track.

2. The first three seconds (the "hold"). Most viewers decide in under two seconds whether to keep watching. If your opening frame is a slow fade from black, a logo or "Hey guys", you lose them before your story starts. Your first frame must already be interesting with the sound off.

3. Sends (shares by DM). Instagram has said that "sends per reach" is one of the strongest signals for showing a reel to people who do not follow you. A video someone sends to a friend with "this is so us" or "what would you do" is worth more than a like.

4. Saves. People save things they want to come back to: tips, recipes, prompts, lists. Saves tell Instagram the content has lasting value.

5. Comments and replies. A comment section that becomes a conversation (people arguing about an answer, guessing a mystery) keeps the reel alive for longer.

6. Likes. Likes still count, but they are the weakest of these signals. Do not optimize for likes alone.

Originality

Instagram has said it reduces distribution for reposted content and for content that is visibly recycled from other apps, such as videos with another platform's watermark. It favors original content. For an AI channel this means:

  • Generate your own visuals. Do not re-upload clips you found.
  • Do not post the same video file again and again.
  • Never upload with a TikTok or CapCut watermark on it.
  • Build a recognizable style so your videos look like yours.

AI labeling

Instagram labels AI-generated or AI-altered content, and asks creators to disclose photorealistic AI media. The Instagram API now has a flag (is_ai_generated) that marks a post as AI-made. Always disclose. Hiding AI use risks warnings and lost reach, and it is not needed. Stylized, clearly fictional AI stories ("In this town, the rain falls upward") do great while labeled. People do not mind AI; they mind being fooled.

What this means for your format

Put the signals together and you get the design brief for every video in this course:

Signal Design rule
Hold The first frame shows the strangest image of the whole video, with a caption already on screen.
Completion Under 35 seconds. A new shot every 1 to 3 seconds. Something escalates at about 7 s, 17 s and 23 s.
Rewatch The last line flows back into the first line, so the loop feels seamless.
Sends The video contains a question or a moment people want to show a friend.
Comments Leave exactly one question open ("What is under the clock?").
Saves For tutorial niches: give a list, a prompt or a step people need later.

Module 2: Choosing a niche and designing a series format

The single biggest difference between AI channels that grow and AI channels that die is the format. Random videos ("here is a cool AI clip") give the viewer no reason to follow. A series gives them a reason: they want the next episode.

What a format is

A format is a story shape that stays the same while the content changes. Examples:

  • In This Town: "In this town, [impossible rule]." Show the rule, show its cost, show one person breaking it, end on the consequence. Same town every episode.
  • Would You Rather: two impossible choices, a 3-second countdown, a reveal of what each choice really means.
  • Part 2 Voting: a cliffhanger story where viewers vote in comments on what the character does next. The next episode follows the winning vote.
  • Secret Rule: a place with a hidden rule. The viewer has to guess it from clues before the reveal.
  • History in 30 seconds: one strange true event, told as a story with a twist ending.
  • What it costs: the hidden price of an everyday thing, shown as a mini-documentary.
  • AI tries to: an AI character attempts a human job and fails in a funny, specific way.
  • Tiny worlds: a miniature world (inside a teacup, under a bed) with its own daily life.

A good format has five properties:

  1. One-sentence pitch. You can explain it in one sentence a 10-year-old would repeat.
  2. Instant recognition. The first two seconds tell a returning viewer "oh, it's this series."
  3. Infinite episodes. You could write 100 episodes without repeating yourself.
  4. A built-in question. Each episode leaves something to talk about in the comments.
  5. Producible with AI. The visuals can be generated in a consistent style without needing real footage.

Picking your niche

Your niche is the "world" your format lives in. Pick the intersection of three circles:

  • What you can produce well with AI. Stylized worlds, fantasy, animals, history, mystery, space and miniature worlds are easy. Realistic people talking to camera is hard, and riskier.
  • What people already watch. Search the topic on Instagram. You want big accounts in the space (proof of demand), but not 1,000 accounts posting the identical thing.
  • What you can monetize later. Some niches lead naturally to products, such as courses, printables, games, books or services. Others only lead to brand deals. See Module 11.

Strong AI niches right now: - eerie or cozy story series - "what if" history - science and space facts with a story twist - tiny worlds and miniatures - animal stories - motivational stories with a twist - explainers for a specific job or hobby - AI-made game worlds tied to a real game you built

The Series Bible

Once you have the format, write a series bible. This one-page document keeps every episode consistent. The pipeline reads it every time it writes an episode. It contains:

  • Series name and one-sentence pitch.
  • The hook template, e.g. "In this town, ...".
  • The beat sheet: the timed structure of every episode (see below).
  • The world: the name of the place, 3 to 5 landmarks, the weather and time of day, the color palette.
  • Recurring characters: a name, one visual trait and one personality trait each.
  • The art style: one paragraph you paste into every image prompt (Module 5).
  • The voice: who is narrating, how fast, what mood.
  • The rules of the series: what you never do (no gore, no real people, no cruelty, and so on).
  • Episode log: one line per published episode, so you never repeat a rule or contradict yourself.

The beat sheet: the most important tool in this course

Here is the real beat sheet behind In This Town. It targets 28 seconds:

  1. 0.0–2.5 s, RULE. "In this town, [rule]." The widest, strangest image of the rule. The on-screen caption is the rule.
  2. 2.5–7 s, PROOF x3. Three fast shots of ordinary life bent by the rule. Each one is funnier or eerier than the last.
  3. 7–12 s, COST. The small sad or scary price the town pays. Show one detail the viewer notices before the narrator says it.
  4. 12–17 s, THE ONE. A kid, newcomer or shopkeeper who does not follow the rule. Name them and give them one trait.
  5. 17–23 s, THE BREAK. They do the forbidden thing. The music goes silent for half a second first.
  6. 23–27 s, THE TURN. The town reacts in a way that changes what the rule meant. Perhaps it was protecting them, or was never needed, or something has been waiting.
  7. 27–28.5 s, LAST LINE. A line that flows back into "In this town..." so the loop restarts cleanly.

Notice what this does to the signals from Module 1: - Beat 1 wins the hold. - Beats 2 to 6 escalate at 7, 17 and 23 seconds, which protects completion. - Beat 7 creates rewatches. - The open question at the end drives comments.

Every format you design should have a beat sheet this specific. "Hook, story, ending" is not a beat sheet. Timed beats with a job for each one is.

What makes a good rule (or a good premise)

These criteria come from the In This Town prompt. They apply to almost any story format: - It is visual in a single frame. Everyone walks backwards; nobody has a shadow except one kid; rain falls upward at noon. - It is simple enough for a 9-year-old to repeat to a friend, and strange enough that they will. - It has an obvious question inside it: why? since when? what happens if you don't? - It has a cost, shown and not told: taped windows, a town clock with no hands, a line of shoes outside every door. - The mood is wonder, not cruelty. Eerie-cozy beats gory. A bedtime story told with a flashlight.

Module 3: Your tool stack

You do not need expensive tools to start. You need one tool per job, and a habit of using the same tools every time so your output stays consistent. AI tools change monthly, so this module names the job first and gives example tools second. If a tool disappears, replace it with another one that does the same job.

The seven jobs

Job What it does Example tools
1. Writer Turns a premise into a script and shot list Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini
2. Image generator Makes keyframes and character sheets Midjourney, GPT image models, Ideogram, Flux, Higgsfield, Leonardo
3. Video generator Turns keyframes into moving shots Kling, Runway, Veo, Hailuo, Higgsfield, Pika, Luma
4. Voice Narration ElevenLabs, OpenAI TTS, free offline voices such as Piper
5. Music and sound Background music and sound effects Licensed libraries, royalty-free packs, AI music tools with commercial licenses, Instagram's in-app audio

Three ways to build the pipeline

Path A: all-in-app (easiest). Write scripts with a chat AI. Generate images and short clips on a web tool. Narrate with a TTS website. Edit in CapCut. Schedule in Meta Business Suite. Cost: a couple of subscriptions. Time per video: 45 to 90 minutes at first, 30 minutes once practiced.

Path B: semi-automated (recommended). Same tools, but the writer outputs structured JSON (Module 4). That one JSON file feeds every other step, so you copy and paste far less. You batch-produce a week of videos in one sitting. Time per video: 15 to 30 minutes.

Path C: fully coded (what Southvale runs). A small app turns each JSON script into a rendered video automatically: - a 3D or 2D renderer draws the scenes from the shot list - an offline neural voice reads the lines - a sound engine adds effects on cue - ffmpeg mixes the audio to streaming loudness and encodes the MP4 - a scheduler posts through the Instagram API

Time per video: minutes of review. You need some coding comfort, or an AI coding assistant, to set it up. Module 9 shows the architecture so you can build it step by step.

Start with Path A or B. Move to C only once you have a format that works. Automating a format nobody watches just makes bad videos faster.

Technical settings you should know now

  • Resolution: 1080x1920 (9:16 vertical).
  • Frame rate: 30 fps is standard; 24 fps gives a more cinematic feel.
  • Length: 15 to 35 seconds for story formats; up to 90 seconds for tutorials.
  • Codec: H.264 MP4 with AAC audio. That is what every editor exports by default.
  • Loudness: about -14 LUFS integrated. Most platforms normalize to around this level, so mixing there means your voice is not crushed or too quiet.
  • Safe zones: keep captions and faces away from the bottom 20% and right 15% of the frame, where Instagram's buttons and caption text sit.

Module 4: Writing scripts with AI: the prompt system

This is where most AI channels go wrong. They type "write a scary story for a reel" and get a generic, over-explained paragraph. Professional results come from a prompt system: a few prompts that work together, each with one job.

The three layers

  1. The master prompt. The rules that apply to every video: platform, length, pacing, voice, safety and output format. You write it once and rarely change it.
  2. The format prompt. The beat sheet and rules for one series (Module 2). One per format.
  3. The episode input. The premise for today, plus the series state (what happened in earlier episodes).

You send all three together. The model answers with structured JSON, not prose.

Why JSON?

A JSON script is a list of shots. Each shot holds its own: - duration - narration line - on-screen caption - image prompt - camera move - sound cue

Because it is structured, each part can go straight to the right tool. Image prompts go to the image generator, lines to the voice and captions to the editor. Nothing gets lost or reworded by accident, and code can read it if you automate later.

Here is the shape Southvale uses (simplified):

{
  "schema": "reel.v1",
  "format": "in_this_town",
  "title": "In this town, nobody touches the ground after sunset.",
  "series": {"id": "hollowmere", "episode": 1},
  "voice": {"style": "calm, close, almost whispering", "words": 68},
  "shots": [
    {
      "t": 0.0, "dur": 2.5,
      "line": "In this town, nobody touches the ground after sunset.",
      "caption": "nobody touches the ground after sunset",
      "visual": "wide dusk street, every villager floating a meter up, ankles tied to fence posts with ribbon",
      "camera": "slow push in",
      "sfx": ["wind_low", "rope_creak"]
    }
  ],
  "post": {
    "caption": "Hollowmere, episode 1. ...",
    "hashtags": ["#inthistown", "#eeriestories", "#shortstory"],
    "pinned_comment": "What do you think is waiting on the ground?"
  }
}

Writing the master prompt

A strong master prompt has these sections. The full text is Prompt 3 in the library.

  • Role. "You are the head writer of a short-form animated series." A role with a job beats "you are a helpful assistant."
  • The scoreboard. Tell the model what it is judged on: the 3-second hold rate, completion rate and sends. Models write better when they know the goal.
  • Hard limits. Total length, words per second (about 2.3 to 2.6 spoken words per second for calm narration), maximum shot length and caption length.
  • Style rules. Present tense. No filler intros. No "Have you ever wondered". Show, don't tell. Specific nouns. One unanswered question.
  • Safety rules. No real people, no brands, no gore, nothing sexual, nothing aimed at mocking a group, no medical or financial claims.
  • Self-check. Make the model check its own draft before answering: could a stranger draw the rule after one viewing? Is the cost shown without being said?
  • Output contract. "Answer with JSON only, matching this schema exactly." Then paste the schema.

Writing the format prompt

Paste your beat sheet from Module 2, plus: - what makes a good premise for this format (with 3 good and 3 bad examples) - the voice for this format - the post copy rules: title pattern, pinned comment, call to action - the series logic: what carries over between episodes

The full In This Town format prompt is Prompt 4 in the library. Use it as a template for your own.

The episode input

Each day you send: - the premise (one line, or "invent one"), - the series state: the town name, the landmarks used so far, the recurring characters, and the last 10 rules used, so the model never repeats itself, - optional: comments from the last episode, if your format uses audience votes.

Getting better outputs: the critique loop

Never use the first draft blindly. Run it through the critic prompt (Prompt 7), which scores the script 1 to 10 on: - the hook - clarity - escalation - the twist - the loop - visual specificity

The critic must name the single weakest shot and rewrite it. Feed the critique back to the writer for a revision. One critique round improves most scripts dramatically. Two rounds is usually the limit; after that the script starts to lose its voice.

Common script problems and fixes

Problem Fix
The opening explains instead of showing Ban the first line from containing "is", "was" or "there". Force a visual hook.
Too many words Set a hard word count (60 to 75 for 28 s) and ask the model to count.
Generic visuals ("a spooky town") Require three concrete nouns per visual: material, light source, one odd detail.
A twist that comes from nowhere Require the twist to be set up by a detail in the first 10 seconds.
Everything gets explained "Leave exactly one question unanswered. Do not explain the origin of the rule."
The ending is flat The last line must lead back into the first line of the video.

Module 5: Visuals: consistent characters and a recognizable style

Viewers follow a look as much as a story. If episode 1 is watercolor, episode 2 is photoreal and episode 3 is anime, nobody recognizes your account in the feed. This module is about building one style and holding it.

The style block

Write one paragraph that describes your art style, and paste it into every image prompt unchanged. A good style block covers: - Medium: "stylized 3D animation", "painterly 2D", "claymation", "miniature diorama photographed with a macro lens". - Lighting: "warm dusk key light, cool blue fill, soft rim light, light fog". - Palette: 4 or 5 named colors, e.g. "deep teal, lantern amber, moss green, bone white". - Camera: "35mm lens, low angle, shallow depth of field". - Texture and finish: "subtle film grain, soft bloom, gentle vignette". - Exclusions: "no text, no logos, no watermark, no extra fingers, no photoreal humans".

Example (the Hollowmere style block):

Stylized 3D storybook animation, rounded soft shapes, slightly oversized heads and hands, warm lantern-amber key light at dusk, cool teal fill, soft rim light, thin ground fog, palette of deep teal, lantern amber, moss green and bone white, 35mm lens at child eye height, gentle film grain and bloom, eerie but cozy mood, no text, no logos, no watermark.

Character sheets

For each recurring character, generate a character sheet: front, side and back views on a plain background, with the same outfit and colors. Then: - use the sheet as a reference image in tools that support image references, character references or "elements"; - describe the character the same way, word for word, in every prompt. Example: "Pip, a 9-year-old girl, red knitted scarf, yellow raincoat, one missing front tooth"; - if the tool supports seeds, reuse the seed for the same character.

From keyframes to motion

The most reliable workflow for AI video today is image first, then animate:

  1. Generate a strong keyframe for each shot using your style block.
  2. Pick the best one and fix small problems (inpaint the hands, clean up any text).
  3. Send it to an image-to-video model with a short motion prompt: what moves, how the camera moves, and for how long (usually 3 to 5 seconds).
  4. Trim each clip to the 1 to 3 seconds you need.

Motion prompts should be short and physical. For example: "slow push in, villagers sway gently on their ribbons, fog drifts left, lanterns flicker." Avoid asking for complex action in a single clip. Several simple clips cut together look better than one complex clip that falls apart.

Making it look expensive

A few tricks lift AI visuals from "AI clip" to "animated series": - Consistent color grade. Apply the same LUT or grade to every clip in the editor. - Grain and vignette over the whole video unify clips from different generations. - Punch-in cuts. Cut from a wide shot to a 120% zoom of the same shot on a beat. It feels like a camera operator is reacting. - Motion on stills. If a video model fails, a slow zoom and pan on a great still image works fine for a 2-second beat. - Never show AI artifacts long. If hands or faces warp, cut before it happens.

Action step

Write your style block. Generate a character sheet for your main recurring character. Produce all keyframes for one script from Module 4, then animate them.

Module 6: Voice, music and sound design

Sound is half of a reel. Viewers forgive simple visuals; they do not forgive a voice that sounds robotic or music that drowns out the narrator.

Narration

  • Pick one voice and keep it. Your narrator is a character. Changing the voice is like changing the host of a show.
  • Match the voice to the format. In This Town uses a calm, close, almost whispering storyteller. A facts channel might use an energetic, curious voice.
  • Speed: about 2.3 to 2.6 words per second for storytelling; up to 3 words per second for energetic facts.
  • Write for the ear. Short sentences. One idea per line. Read the script aloud before generating.
  • Use pauses. Many TTS tools respect punctuation: a period gives a short pause and an ellipsis a longer one. Put a half-second of silence right before the twist.

Music

  • Use one music bed per format, with variations, so the music itself becomes part of the recognition.
  • Mood over melody. Low, simple, atmospheric beds work under narration. Busy melodies fight the voice.
  • Licensing matters. Only use music you have the right to use:
  • royalty-free libraries whose license covers social media
  • AI music tools whose terms grant you commercial rights
  • Instagram's in-app audio library (added inside the Instagram app) Never rip songs from YouTube or Spotify. Trending audio can only be added inside the Instagram app, not through the API.

Sound effects

Sound effects make AI visuals feel real. Add one at each important moment: - a whoosh on fast cuts - a low boom on the rule reveal - ambient room tone (wind, crowd murmur, rain) under everything - a silence drop right before the twist, then a single sharp sound

The Southvale pipeline writes sound cues into the JSON script ("sfx": ["wind_low", "rope_creak"]). The editor or code then places them automatically. You can do the same by hand: read the sfx field of each shot and drop the matching effect at that time.

The mix

  1. Voice is king. Everything else sits under it.
  2. Ducking: lower the music by about 8 to 12 dB whenever the voice is speaking. CapCut and most editors can do this automatically. In code, ffmpeg's sidechaincompress filter does it.
  3. Loudness: normalize the final mix to about -14 LUFS integrated, with peaks under -1 dBTP.
  4. Check on a phone speaker, not just headphones. Most viewers watch on a phone, often with low volume.

Action step

Generate the narration for your two scripts. Pick one music bed for your format and build a small sound-effect folder of about 20 sounds. Mix one video completely.

Module 1: How Instagram Reels actually get distributed

You cannot design videos that spread until you understand what the platform is measuring. Instagram does not publish its full ranking formula. It has, however, said publicly which signals matter most for Reels. Those signals line up with what every successful short-form creator has learned the hard way.

The signals that matter

1. Watch time and completion. Did people watch the whole thing? Did they watch it twice? A 20-second reel watched to the end by 70% of viewers will beat a 60-second reel watched to the end by 15%. This is the most important number you will track.

2. The first three seconds (the "hold"). Most viewers decide in under two seconds whether to keep watching. If your opening frame is a slow fade from black, a logo or "Hey guys", you lose them before your story starts. Your first frame must already be interesting with the sound off.

3. Sends (shares by DM). Instagram has said that "sends per reach" is one of the strongest signals for showing a reel to people who do not follow you. A video someone sends to a friend with "this is so us" or "what would you do" is worth more than a like.

4. Saves. People save things they want to come back to: tips, recipes, prompts, lists. Saves tell Instagram the content has lasting value.

5. Comments and replies. A comment section that becomes a conversation (people arguing about an answer, guessing a mystery) keeps the reel alive for longer.

6. Likes. Likes still count, but they are the weakest of these signals. Do not optimize for likes alone.

Originality

Instagram has said it reduces distribution for reposted content and for content that is visibly recycled from other apps, such as videos with another platform's watermark. It favors original content. For an AI channel this means:

  • Generate your own visuals. Do not re-upload clips you found.
  • Do not post the same video file again and again.
  • Never upload with a TikTok or CapCut watermark on it.
  • Build a recognizable style so your videos look like yours.

AI labeling

Instagram labels AI-generated or AI-altered content, and asks creators to disclose photorealistic AI media. The Instagram API now has a flag (is_ai_generated) that marks a post as AI-made. Always disclose. Hiding AI use risks warnings and lost reach, and it is not needed. Stylized, clearly fictional AI stories ("In this town, the rain falls upward") do great while labeled. People do not mind AI; they mind being fooled.

What this means for your format

Put the signals together and you get the design brief for every video in this course:

Signal Design rule
Hold The first frame shows the strangest image of the whole video, with a caption already on screen.
Completion Under 35 seconds. A new shot every 1 to 3 seconds. Something escalates at about 7 s, 17 s and 23 s.
Rewatch The last line flows back into the first line, so the loop feels seamless.
Sends The video contains a question or a moment people want to show a friend.
Comments Leave exactly one question open ("What is under the clock?").
Saves For tutorial niches: give a list, a prompt or a step people need later.
Originality Same characters, same world and same art style every episode.

Action step

Open Instagram and watch 30 reels in the niche you are considering. For each one, write down: - What was on screen in the first frame? - Did you watch to the end? - Did you want to send it to someone?

You will start to see the patterns from the table above everywhere.

The beat sheet

The beat sheet: the most important tool in this course

Here is the real beat sheet behind In This Town. It targets 28 seconds:

  1. 0.0–2.5 s, RULE. "In this town, [rule]." The widest, strangest image of the rule. The on-screen caption is the rule.
  2. 2.5–7 s, PROOF x3. Three fast shots of ordinary life bent by the rule. Each one is funnier or eerier than the last.
  3. 7–12 s, COST. The small sad or scary price the town pays. Show one detail the viewer notices before the narrator says it.
  4. 12–17 s, THE ONE. A kid, newcomer or shopkeeper who does not follow the rule. Name them and give them one trait.
  5. 17–23 s, THE BREAK. They do the forbidden thing. The music goes silent for half a second first.
  6. 23–27 s, THE TURN. The town reacts in a way that changes what the rule meant. Perhaps it was protecting them, or was never needed, or something has been waiting.
  7. 27–28.5 s, LAST LINE. A line that flows back into "In this town..." so the loop restarts cleanly.

Notice what this does to the signals from Module 1: - Beat 1 wins the hold. - Beats 2 to 6 escalate at 7, 17 and 23 seconds, which protects completion. - Beat 7 creates rewatches. - The open question at the end drives comments.

Every format you design should have a beat sheet this specific. "Hook, story, ending" is not a beat sheet. Timed beats with a job for each one is.

5 free prompts

Prompt 1: Series Bible builder

You are the showrunner of a short-form animated series for Instagram Reels. Build a complete
series bible from my idea below. The series must work as 20-35 second vertical videos made
entirely with AI images, AI video clips and AI narration.

My idea: [ONE OR TWO SENTENCES ABOUT YOUR SERIES]
My audience: [WHO WATCHES, e.g. "teens and adults who like eerie-cozy stories"]

Write these sections, using short, concrete language:
1. Series name and a one-sentence pitch a 10-year-old could repeat.
2. The hook template every episode opens with (max 6 words before the variable part).
3. A timed beat sheet for a 28-second episode: 6-8 beats, each with start-end seconds and
   the job of that beat. Include escalation points.
4. The world: name, 5 landmarks (each with one visual detail), weather, time of day,
   a 5-color palette with names.
5. Three recurring characters: name, age, one visual trait, one personality trait,
   what they want.
6. An art style block of 60-90 words I can paste into every image prompt (medium,
   lighting, palette, lens, texture, exclusions).
7. Narrator: voice, pace in words per second, mood, three example lines.
8. "Never do" rules: at least 8.
9. Ten example episode premises in one line each.
10. One sentence explaining why viewers will follow for the next episode.

Prompt 2: Premise generator

You write premises for the series "[SERIES NAME]". Format: [PASTE YOUR HOOK TEMPLATE AND
ONE-SENTENCE PITCH].

Generate 25 new premises. Each premise must be:
- visual in a single frame (someone could draw it after hearing it once),
- simple enough for a 9-year-old to repeat, strange enough that they would,
- carrying an obvious question (why? since when? what if you don't?),
- carrying a hidden cost that can be SHOWN, not told,
- eerie, funny or wondrous, never cruel, gory or sad.

Do not reuse any of these earlier premises: [PASTE YOUR EPISODE LOG]

Output a numbered list. For each: the premise in one line, then "Cost:" with the visual
cost, then "Question:" with the question it leaves open. Then pick your top 5 and explain
in one sentence each why they will stop the scroll.

Prompt 3: Master prompt (paste above every script request)

ROLE: You are the head writer of a short-form animated series published as Instagram Reels.
You are judged on three numbers: the 3-second hold rate, the completion rate and sends
per view. Everything you write serves those numbers.

HARD LIMITS
- Total length: [24-32] seconds. Narration [60-75] words (about 2.4 words per second).
- No shot longer than 3 seconds. Escalations at about 7 s, 17 s and 23 s.
- On-screen captions: max 6 words each.
- Present tense. Short sentences. No filler openings ("Have you ever", "Imagine",
  "Did you know", "Hey guys").
- The first line IS the hook and must show something strange, not explain it.
- Leave exactly one question unanswered.
- The final line must flow back into the first line so the video loops.

STYLE
- Show, don't tell. Concrete nouns: materials, light sources, one odd detail per shot.
- Every visual description is 15-35 words and includes subject, action, setting,
  light and one odd detail.
- Keep characters and places consistent with the SERIES STATE below.

SAFETY
- No real people, brands, logos, celebrities, politicians or copyrighted characters.
- No gore, sexual content, slurs, self-harm, dangerous stunts, medical or money claims.
- Suitable for a general audience.

SELF-CHECK before answering
1. Could a stranger describe the premise after one viewing?
2. Is the cost shown without being said?
3. Does the twist pay off a detail planted in the first 10 seconds?
4. Does the last line hand back to the first?
5. Is there exactly one open question?
Fix any "no" before you answer.

Prompt 7: The critic

You are a ruthless short-form video editor. Score this reel script from 1-10 on each:
hook (first 2 seconds), clarity, escalation, twist set-up and payoff, loop,
visual specificity, word count vs. length, and comment potential.

Then:
1. Name the single weakest shot and explain why in one sentence.
2. Rewrite that shot.
3. List up to 3 other concrete changes, each under 20 words.
4. Give an overall verdict: PUBLISH, REVISE or KILL.

Do not praise. Do not rewrite the whole script.
